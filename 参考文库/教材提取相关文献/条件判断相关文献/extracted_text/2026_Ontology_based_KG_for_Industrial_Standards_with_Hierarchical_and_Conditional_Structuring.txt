2 Related Works 123
2.1 Ontology 124
Previous studies have defined an ontology as a for- 125
mal representation of core concepts used within a 126
specific domain and the relationships among those 127
concepts (Miller, 1995; Ashburner et al., 2000). 128
Building on this definition, research on ontology 129
learning, which aims to automatically construct on- 130
tologies within a domain, has primarily evolved 131
around concept discovery and relation extraction. 132
More recently, attempts have emerged to leverage 133
LLMs to expand schemas without predefined struc- 134
tures or to generate ontologies in an end-to-end 135
manner (Funk et al., 2023; Lo et al., 2025). How- 136
ever, while these approaches mainly focus on lexi- 137
cal or taxonomic relationships (e.g., is-a relations), 138
this study distinguishes itself from prior ontology 139
research by proposing a domain-specific ontology 140
that explicitly reflects the hierarchical structure and 141
rule-oriented concepts inherent in industrial stan- 142
dards documents. 143
2.2 Knowledge Graph 144
KG represents entities and relations in the form of 145
triplets, enabling structured knowledge storage and 146
reasoning. Early extracted triplets using rule-based 147
or statistical methods (Etzioni et al., 2004; Yates 148
et al., 2007), and the advent of neural models signif- 149
icantly improved relation extraction performance at 150
the sentence level (Wei et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2018). 151
More recently, LLM-based approaches have been 152
proposed to extract triplets or to construct KGs via 153
prompt-driven methods that can be applied across 154
diverse domains (Huguet Cabot and Navigli, 2021; 155
Zhang and Soh, 2024b; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 156
2
2025). However, while these approaches are effec-157
tive at modeling simple sentence structures, they158
struggle to adequately represent compound sen-159
tences in which multiple conditions are nested or160
combined. In contrast, this study structures intra-161
sentence conditions and their logical compositions162
based on an ontology, and constructs a KG that163
reflects these structures, thereby enabling the accu-164
rate modeling of complex sentences with multiple165
overlapping conditions.166
3 Method167
This section presents a pipeline for transforming in-168
dustrial standards documents into structured knowl-169
edge by formalizing the document’s hierarchical170
structure as an ontology and extracting triplets171
based on condition–consequence rules. The pro-172
posed ontology is designed to jointly represent the173
document hierarchy and conditional rules, and the174
overall pipeline consists of three stages, as illus-175
trated in Figure 2.176
3.1 Hierarchical Ontology Modeling177
Industrial standards documents have a hi-178
erarchical structure organized into SEC-179
TION –SUBSECTION –CLAUSE levels, and180
the meanings of various elements, such as tables,181
conditional statements, and exception rules, are de-182
termined by this hierarchical context. Accordingly,183
this study defines each document section as a core184
concept and constructs an ontology that captures185
the relationships among sections. To this end, we186
design the following two-step procedure.187
Hierarchical Structural Decomposition of Doc-188
uments Sections, clauses, tables, and footnotes189
in industrial standards documents are identified,190
and heterogeneous numbering schemes are normal-191
ized using rule-based methods to form a consistent192
hierarchical structure. Tables and footnotes are as-193
signed to their corresponding sections based on194
their positions, thereby preserving the logical struc-195
ture of the document. Through this process, the196
document is represented as the following hierarchi-197
cal ontology structure:198
H = {Section → Subsection → Subsubsection
→ (Text ∪ Table) → Footnote}. (1)199
This hierarchical structure enables the valid scope200
of each rule to be constrained in subsequent stages,201
providing a foundation for preserving the structural202
semantics unique to industrial standards documents 203
without loss. 204
Knowledge Unit Construction Based on the nor- 205
malized hierarchical structure, we transform the 206
document into knowledge units Ui in the form of 207
title–text pairs: 208
D = {U1, U2, . . . , UN }, U i = (t(i)
title, t(i)
text), (2) 209
where N denotes the total number of title–text pairs 210
generated according to the document’s hierarchi- 211
cal structure. The title serves as an anchor that 212
represents the hierarchical context (e.g., section, 213
subsection, or clause) to which the rule belongs. 214
The text contains the rule content, including de- 215
scriptions, table cell entries, conditional statements, 216
and numerical constraints within the corresponding 217
section (see Figure 3). This structure preserves the 218
rule applicability scope during triplet extraction, 219
thereby capturing the characteristics of industrial 220
standards documents in which the meaning of the 221
same term varies depending on the rule’s valid 222
scope. 223

Modeling 225

columns define conditions and the corresponding 231
cell values represent consequences, forming a uni- 232

limited in their ability to represent such conditional 235
relationships. To address this limitation, we intro- 236
duce Sentence-level Conditional Structure Mod- 237
eling, which explicitly incorporates conditional 238
characteristics into the triplet extraction process. 239

3.3 Ontology-based KG Construction 286
In this section, a three-stage procedure is applied 287
to consolidate redundant expressions from the pre- 288
viously extracted triplets and to remove structural 289
noise, resulting in a coherent KG. 290
3.3.1 Entity–Relation Synonym Dictionary 291
Construction 292
To ensure conceptual consistency by unifying syn- 293
onymous expressions of entities and relations, we 294
construct a synonym dictionary to normalize rep- 295
resentations within the KG. In this study, entities 296
and relations are clustered by jointly considering 297
not only text similarity but also contextual and 298
graph-structural information. For relations, we fur- 299
ther apply selectional preference based on the sub- 300
ject–object entity type distributions. The centroid 301
of each cluster is selected as the canonical represen- 302
tation to normalize the KG. Detailed construction 303
procedures are provided in Appendix D. 304

3.3.3 Schema Design for Integrated 341
Ontology-based KG 342
We design a connection schema to integrate the 343
hierarchical ontology of documents and the KG 344
into a unified graph structure. Each section in a 345
document is defined as a structural unit, and the 346
containment relationship between parent and child 347
sections is modeled using the [has_child] re- 348
lation. In addition, to explicitly connect content- 349
based knowledge contained within each section, 350
we define a [mention] relation between section 351
nodes and KG entity nodes referenced in the cor- 352
responding section. This relation aligns individual 353
knowledge entities with specific structural loca- 354
tions in the document, enabling a linkage between 355
contextual positional information and Ontology- 356
based structural information that is not provided by 357
conventional KGs. The resulting Ontology-based 358
KG, which unifies hierarchical document structures 359
with content-based knowledge, is implemented in 360
the graph database management systemNeo4j us- 361
ing the proposed integrated schema. 362

Limitations590
The proposed approach is optimized for documents591
with explicit structural cues, such as hierarchical592
sections, conditional rules, and tables, which ef-593
fectively stabilize LLM-based reasoning. Conse-594
quently, its direct applicability to fully unstruc-595
tured open-domain texts, where such structural sig-596
nals are scarce, may be limited. Nevertheless, the597
pipeline is not confined to industrial standards and598
can be applied to other rule-centric documents with599
explicit structural units, including technical manu-600
als, legal or contractual texts, and policy documents.601
Moreover, the proposed Sentence-level Conditional602
Structure Modeling provides a general mechanism603
for decomposing complex nested conditions and604
multi-exception rules into logical units, making it605
applicable across domains with complex rule struc-606
tures. Finally, as the approach relies on LLM-based607
knowledge extraction, its performance depends on608
model stability. Future work will explore training609
domain-specific local models using the extracted610
ontology and rule patterns, thereby reducing re-611
liance on external LLMs and improving reliability,612
reproducibility, and operational robustness.613

A.1 Ontology-based MindMap797
Ontology-based MindMap integrates the original798
MindMap pipeline with the Ontology-based KG,799
such that the entire process of evidence retrieval800
and reasoning is constrained by the hierarchical801
document structure and rule-level semantic con-802
text (see Figure 6). The proposed methodology con-803
sists of four stages: Entity Extraction, Ontology-804
Constrained Evidence Graph Mining, Evidence805
Graph Aggregation, and LLM-integrated Rea-806
soning.807
In the Entity Extraction stage, key domain en-808
tity candidates are identified from the query using809
an LLM-based extractor. Rather than being used810
directly, the extracted entities are mapped to actual811
KG entities via cosine similarity matching with pre-812
constructed Ontology-based KG entity embeddings.813
The resulting entity set serves as anchor points for814
subsequent graph exploration.815
In the Ontology-Constrained Evidence Graph816
Mining stage, an evidence graph is explored start-817
ing from the matched entities, while all explo-818
ration processes are strictly constrained within819
the hierarchical ontology structure of the docu-820
ment. Specifically, candidate evidence is restricted821
to query-relevant document sections by leverag-822
ing the [has_child] and [mention] relations,823
effectively suppressing structural noise that may824
arise from global KG traversal. Neighbor search825
is then performed to collect local rules and en- 826
tity relations, followed by shortest-path search 827
between entity pairs. During this process, ontol- 828
ogy relations that represent document structure 829
are excluded from the reasoning paths, ensur- 830
ing that only substantive rule-level and seman- 831
tic relations are used as reasoning evidence. In 832
particular, rules extracted from tables and con- 833
ditional statements are preferentially collected 834
in a form that preserves condition–consequence 835
structures via the[has_condition_AND/_OR] and 836
[has_consequence_AND/_OR] relations. 837
In the Evidence Graph Aggregation stage, the 838
retrieved triplets are unified into a single reason- 839
ing graph through duplicate removal and struc- 840
tural normalization. Rather than simply enumer- 841
ating triplets, evidence is aggregated at the level 842
of condition–consequence units (i.e., case-level ag- 843
gregation) to prevent fragmentation of the logical 844
semantics of rules. The resulting reasoning graph 845
thus simultaneously reflects both the hierarchical 846
document context and the underlying rule struc- 847
tures. 848
Finally, in the LLM-integrated Reasoning 849
stage, the reasoning graph is injected into the LLM 850
prompt to perform question answering. The LLM 851
conducts reasoning along ontology-refined evi- 852
dence paths, suppressing structure-agnostic infer- 853
ence and generating consistent answers grounded 854
in document structure. Through this process, 855
Ontology-based MindMap realizes a collaborative 856
reasoning pipeline that combines the structural 857
strengths of KGs with the language-based reason- 858
ing capabilities of LLMs. 859
A.2 Ontology-based KG-Retriever 860

Table-based triplet Extraction Prompt
Instruction
You are an expert in constructing knowledge graphs. Your task is to extract knowledge triplets (subject–relation–object)
from the given tabular content. The input content may include:
• Tabular content (structured tables with conditions and permitted variations)
• Table titles
• Table captions appearing outside the table
Output Format
• Use the following structure for each triplet:
<triplet> {subject} <subj> {object} <obj> {relation_name}
• <subj> and <obj> are explicit boundary markers indicating the end of the subject and object spans, respectively.
They are not natural language tokens but structural delimiters.
Guidelines (Excerpt)
1. Extract only semantically meaningful and domain-relevant relations.
2. For enumerations or numerical constraints:
• Capture requirement constraints (e.g., thickness < 3 mm).
• Normalize all numerical values into SI units (e.g., millimeters, Celsius), even if the input uses mixed units.
3. For tabular data with conditions:
• If the input starts with the | character, treat it strictly as table data: do not apply any plain text rules, and only use
table-specific rules.
• When processing tables, the ONLY allowed relations are:
– If there is only one condition → has_condition
– If there are multiple conditions → has_condition_AND,has_condition_OR
– If there is only one consequence → has_consequence
– If there are multiple consequences → has_consequence_AND,has_consequence_OR
– Do not use any other relation names.
• Each cell in a table must be represented as a rule node (caseX).
– For every non-empty cell, create a new case node (case1,case2, . . . ).
– Each case node must include:
* all row labels as conditions,
* all column labels as conditions,
* and the cell value as the consequence.
• When logical expressions contain both AND and OR, you must introduce a logic_groupX node.
– Inside alogic_groupX, you must use ONLY one type of edge: eitherhas_condition_AND,has_condition_OR,
has_consequence_AND, orhas_consequence_OR.
– Thelogic_groupX itself must then connect to the case node (caseX) using the appropriate relation among the
same four types.
– Always preserve operator precedence by grouping first.
Example: (A OR B) AND C → A and B are both connected to a logic_groupX with has_condition_OR; then
thislogic_groupX and C are connected tocaseX with has_condition_AND.
– Always include the column/row name together with the cell value when forming a condition or consequence.
4. Always include the corresponding row or column name when forming a condition or consequence.
5. Do not directly connect conditions to consequences; always connect them through a caseX orlogic_groupX.
6. Keep relation names consistent and in snake_case.
7. Do not generate vague or trivial relations.

Instruction

from the given document section. The input content may include:
• Plain text (technical specifications, requirements, descriptions)

• Enumerated content (lists, measurement conversions)
• Tabular content (structured tables with conditions and permitted variations)
Output Format
• Use the following structure for each triplet:
<triplet> {subject} <subj> {object} <obj> {relation_name}
• <subj> and <obj> are explicit boundary markers indicating the end of the subject and object spans, respectively.
They are not natural language tokens but structural delimiters.
Guidelines (Excerpt)
1. Extract only semantically meaningful and domain-relevant relations.
2. For plain text:
• Coverage or scope → (covers, applies_to)
• Process or production → (produced_by, used_for)
• Definitions → (stands_for, defined_as)
• Specifications → (has_condition_AND, has_condition_OR, has_condition, has_consequence, has_consequence_OR,

• Variable definitions → (X <subj> Y <obj> stands_for)
• Formula representation → (X <subj> formula <obj> calculated_by)
• Component terms → (X <subj> Y <obj> includes_component)
• Operations → (X <subj> Y <obj> divided_by / multiplied_by / added_to / subtracted_by)

• Capture requirement constraints (e.g., thickness ≥ 3 mm)
• Always normalize numerical values into SI units (millimeters, Celsius, etc.) in the output triplets, even if the input
uses mixed units.
5. When text starts with a numbering pattern (e.g., "1.", "2.1", "3.2.4"), do not extract that numeric label as a triplet. Only
process the semantic content that follows.

7. Use technical terms exactly as written in the input text.
8. Do not generate vague or trivial relations.
9. Do NOT output placeholder tokens such as "<relation>" or generic words like "relation". Always replace with an actual
relation name (e.g., covers, produced_by, equivalent_to).
